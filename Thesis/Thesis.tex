	\subsection{Design Parameters}	\label{ss:des_para}
		
		% Geometric Design Variablees
	
		The wingspan and hub height are two parameters of the UAV frame that were not selected as design variables. The inner hub height is set to 64mm, which represents the minimum height required to fit the proposed battery between the two plates. It should be noted that the actual batteries would not fit on this frame model, because the hub web would intersect the battery. Further work could be done to refine the mesh, allowing for more realistic battery storage. 
		
		The wingspan was set as a parameter due to the fact that increasing beyond the minimum will not increase the objective function, as increasing the wingspan increases mass and the mass moment of inertia while also causing more stress on the vehicle. The distance from the center of the UAV to the propeller is calculated to be 1127.3mm. This is calculated by finding the minimum distance required to fit all 8 propellers with a radius of 406.4mm with a propeller spacing of 25mm.

This model assumes that the entire mass of the frame is concentrated at the vehicle’s center. This method overestimates the stress in the frame due to all the mass being modeled at a single point. 	\section{Mission Profile} \label{se:mission_profile}
	
		A model of the mission profile was created to represent a potential mission with continuous position, velocity, and acceleration as functions of time. The mission is defined as a series of connected profile segments, with constraints imposed on position, velocity, and acceleration at the nodes between segments. Any unconstrained nodal values are calculated using basic kinematic relationships. Between segment nodes, position, velocity, and acceleration are interpolated using cubic Hermite splines, resulting in acceleration that varies linearly with respect to time.
		
		The mission profile is based on a potential operation during the 2025 Palisades wildfire. The total mission distance is estimated to be 5600m, corresponding to the distance from the northern edge to the southern edge of the active fire area. Using elevation maps of the surrounding terrain, the required altitude change to traverse this path is estimated to be 460m \cite{MapViewer}. Timesteps were selected such that the maximum downrange velocity remained below 12m/s.
		
		The climb, cruise, and descent phases are each described using two cubic Hermite spline intervals to avoid over-constraining the mission profile. Figure \ref{fig:xvy} shows altitude as a function of downrange position, Figure \ref{fig:yvt} shows altitude versus time, and Figure \ref{fig:xvt} shows downrange position with respect to time. The constraints applied to the mission profile are summarized in Table \ref{tab:profcons}, where the duration represents the time between spline intervals. Blank entries indicate that no constraint was applied.

	\subsection{Thrust Calculation}\label{ss:thrust}
		
		A calculation of the thrust profile is required in order to preform the power and fatigue analysis. This is the stress-equivalent force over the duration of the mission profile with drag and acceleration taken into account. The thrust equation over the duration of the mission profile is shown in Equation \ref{e:thrust}. 
		
		\begin{equation}
			F_{\text{thrust}}(t)
			=
			\pm v(t)^2 \, s_a \, \rho \, c_d
			+
			m\bigl(a(t)+g\bigr)
			\label{eq:thrust}
		\end{equation}
		
		The v(t) and a(t) represent the velocity and acceleration of the mission profile at given point in time. $s_a$ is an estimate of the surface area of UAV. This is obtained by summing the projected surface of every beam and hub segment with the projected surface area of the two batteries. The surface area is a function of the design variables. $\rho = 1.2250\unit{\kilogram\per\meter\cubed}$ is the air density. $c_d = 0.256$ is the drag coefficient. $g = 9.8066\unit{\meter\second\squared}$ was used for the gravity constant. The mass is estimated as the sum of 45kg payload, the mass of the frame, and the mass of the two batteries.
		
		To simulate the pre-liftoff and post-landing conditions, a rev-up and rev-down period were added to the thrust forcing function. The ramp-up phase is modeled as a linear, increasing thrust from zero to the thrust required for takeoff, while the ramp-down phase is modeled as a linear decrease in thrust to zero. The rev down period was modeled as a linearly decreasing force until no force is applied. A duration of five seconds is used for both periods.

		\subsection{Energy Consumption Calculation} \label{ss:energy_consumption_calc}
		
		The energy consumed over the mission duration is computed by subtracting the time integral of the power draw from the total energy capacity of the battery system. Equation \ref{eq:p_to_e} describes the relationship between the energy remaining in the battery and the power consumed. $E_{Remaining}(t)$ is the total energy remaining between both batteries as a function of time. $E_{Capacity}$ is the energy stored by a fully charged battery. $t$ is the time since the start of the mission.  
		
		
		\begin{equation} \label{eq:p_to_e}
			E_{\text{Remaining}}(t) = E_{\text{Capacity}} - \int_{0}^{t} P(t) \, dt
		\end{equation}
		
		
		The integral of the power is calculated numerically using scipy.integrate.cumulative{\_}trapezoid. The number of time steps was is a number set when running the optimization function. The total span of time calculated is the duration of the mission.

From these cross section dimensions, the relevant properties are calculated \cite{StructXannulus}\cite{StructXibeam} such as: cross-sectional area, second moment of area in all 3 axes, torsional constant, transverse shear deflection constant in both direction on the cross section plane, and the circumscribed radius of the cross section.

		\subsection{Beam Element Formulation} \label{se:beam_element_form}
		
			% Description	
			For each beam element a stiffness and mass matrix within the local coordinate system must be created. The stiffness matrix uses Timoshenko beam theory so shear deformation effects are taken into account. This creates a more accurate model than Euler–Bernoulli beam which is desired for the use in the transient and dynamic analysis. The local stiffness matrix has 12 degrees of freedom. It has two nodes, each with three displacement and three rotational degrees of freedom. Timoshenko beam theory was selected to account for transverse shear deformation, which allows for more accuracy than Euler–Bernoulli beam theory for the dynamic and transient analyses performed in this study. The stiffness matrix requires the length of the beam. It also requires cross section properties such as area, second moments of area, the torsional constant, and the transverse shear deflection constant. The material properties required are the elastic modulus and shear modulus. 
			
			The mass matrix is a three-dimensional lumped mass matrix. This requires the total mass of the beam, which is calculated by multiplying the length, the density, and the cross section area. The mass matrix also requires the mass moment of inertia tensor in the local coordinate system. The annulus cross section uses the mass moment of inertia tensor for a thick-walled cylindrical tube \cite{MassMoment}. The I-beam uses mass moment of inertia tensors of three rectangular cuboids \cite{MassMoment} assembled with parallel-axis theorem.

The boundary condition value is the direction or rotation is set to.

This requires a vector in the global coordinate system where the x-direction of the boundary condition should be placed in and a k-node vector which determines the y-direction. The directions of the resulting coordinate system is the transformation matrix. If the boundary condition is applied in the global coordinate system, the transformation matrix is set to the identity matrix.

Three amplitudes are stored for each direction in three-dimensional space and three amplitudes are stored for each axis of rotation. The oscillating stress amplitude is calculated for each element by substituting the assumed displacements in the stress solver and instead solving for the stress solve for the stress amplitude. 	\section{Beam Stresses}
		
		
		Once the global stresses are calculated, they are transformed back into the local coordinate space so that the shape functions can be calculated. The shape functions used are three dimensional and $C^1$ continuous. \cite{feabook} The shape functions require a specific value of x, representing the distance along the beam in local coordinates. The shape functions allow for displacement and rotations to be interpolated at any point within the beam. 
		
		Using the shape functions, the bending stress can be calculated at any point along the beam. The formula used is $\sigma_b(x)=-hE\frac{d\theta(x)}{dx}$. $\sigma_b(x)$ is the maximum bending stress in the beam in a given principle bending axis. $\frac{d\theta(x)}{dx}$ Is the derivative of rotation along the beam axis obtained from the shape functions. 
		
		$E$ is the Young's Modulus. $h$ is the radius of circumscribed cross section. This causes an overestimation of the stress and was selected to simplify the calculation. This causes the stress constraint to be more stringent than reality. The bending stress is calculated for both bending axes and the resulting maximum stress is taken by taking Euclidean norm of both vectors.

		The mass moment of inertia tensor for the UAV frame is calculated. This is used in the objective function of the optimization problem. The calculation only takes into account the mass moment of the UAV frame, and not the mass moment of the batteries or the payload. % Ask about this?? 
		
		The mass moment of the system is calculated by summing the mass moment of each beam in the finite element model. The calculation for mass moment has the hub subdivided into individual I-beams. The principal moments of inertia are calculated by taking the eigenvalues of the tensor. The eigenvalues are solved using np.linalg.eig.

	\subsection{Objective Function} \label{ss:desvar}
		
		The objective function is formulated in order to both minimize the mass and mass moment of inertia of the UAV frame. The objective function is described in Equation \ref{eq:objective_function}. $M_{\text{frame}}$ is the mass of the frame given a set of design variables. $I_{\text{frame}}$ is the mass moment of inertia of the frame for a given set of design variables. Scaling factors $S_i$ and $S_m$ were added to keep both mass and mass moment on the same order of magnitude. The scaling factor for mass ($S_m$) is set to $10^3$ and the scaling factor for mass moment of inertia $10^-3$. The weighting factor ($W_m$) for mass is 2/3 and the weighting factor for mass moment of inertia ($W_i$) is 1/3.

			J(X) =
			\frac{W_mM_{\text{frame}}(X)}{S_m}
			+
			\frac{W_iI_{\text{frame}}(X)}{S_i}
			\label{eq:objective_function}
		\end{equation}

			\label{fig:dfd_disp}
		\end{figure}
		 
		
		The formula for the displacement constraint is shown in Equation \ref{eq:opt_disp}. $d_{allowable}$ is the maximum allowable displacement.
		
		 \begin{equation}
			g_1(X) = d_{\max} - d_{\text{allowable}} \le 0
			\label{eq:opt_disp}
		\end{equation}
		
		
	 	$\sigma_{yeild}$ is the tension yield strength of the material. $\text{FOS}$ is the factor of safety associated with the stress constraint. Factor of safety is a parameter of the analysis. 
		
		\begin{equation}
			g_2(X) = \sigma_{\max} - \frac{\sigma_{\text{yield}}}{\text{FOS}} \le 0
			\label{eq:opt_stress}
		\end{equation}
		
	\subsubsection{Frequency} \label{sss:frquency}
	
		
		The objective of the frequency constraint is to ensure that the frequency experienced by the propeller is not near the natural frequency of the vehicle. This is accomplished by calculating the maximum admittance over a range of values.
		
		The design flow diagram for the frequency is shown in Figure \ref{fig:dfd_freq}. First, the beam model of the UAV slice discussed in Subsection \ref{ss:seg_model} is created. This beam model is used for both the dynamic and static solvers. The same force is applied to the static model which has a magnitude equal to the amplitude of the force on dynamic model. 
		
		The dynamic model is run repeatedly with frequencies over a range of values. This range is calculated by from converting finding the maximum and minimum values for thrust on the thrust curve calculated in Subsection \ref{ss:thrust}, then converting those values to minimum and maximum frequency using Equation \ref{eq:ang}.
		
		The rev-up and rev-down sections are not considered for the thrust analysis. The maximum displacement amplitude between all run dynamic models ($d_amplitude$) is found. The maximum displacement in the static model was also found ($d_{static}$). Equation \ref{eq:opt_freq} is the formulation of the constraint. The allowable admittence is represented $Y_{\text{allowable}}$.
		
		\begin{equation}
			g_3(X)
			= \frac{d_{\text{amplitude}}}{d_{\text{static}}} - Y_{\text{allowable}}
			\le 0
			\label{eq:opt_freq}
		\end{equation}

			\centering
			\includegraphics[scale=0.1]{Images/DFDs/DFDFrequency.png}
			\caption{DFD of the Transient Solver} 
			\label{fig:dfd_freq}
		\end{figure}
	
			
	\subsubsection{Damage} \label{sss:damage}
	
		% Explain surface area once	
		
		The damage constraint is imposed in order to limit damage caused by the vibrations in the vehicle. The design flow diagram of the is shown in Figure \ref{fig:dfd_damage}. The mass of the drone is calculated geometrically using model discussed in Section \ref{se:uav_geo}. The mass of the batteries and payload are taken to find the total mass ($m_total$). The projected surface area of the UAV is calculated. This is the sum of the area of the top surface of the UAV hub and the projected surface area of the UAV arms and struts. The mission profile, total mass, and surface area are used to calculate the thrust function shown in Equation \ref{eq:thrust}. The thrust function is transferred to a angular frequency function by applying Equation \ref{eq:ang}. The thrust function is then used to calculate the perturbation force described by Equation \ref{eq:thr}. The beam model used is the slice model discussed in Subsection \ref{ss:seg_model}. Both the perturbation and thrust are upward forces and are applied to the outer node of the slice model. Both forces are divided by eight due to only applying to one slice of the model. 
		
		The number of time-steps for the transient solver is set by a constant set by the user. The constants used for Newmark-beta interrogation are $\beta = 0.25$ and $\gamma = 0.5$. The results for the bending stress are taken for each timestep at the point of maximum bending stress. 
		
		Once the stress function is obtained, the rainflow counting algorithm is applied to calculate the damage. The Rainflow module in the PyLife library is used and it uses the four-point Rainflow-counting algorithm to transfer the time-varying load into sets of constant amplitude stress reversals. This is used with the Woehler curve data to calculate the total damage accumulated over the mission. This is done using the LoadCollective class in the PyLife library. 
		
		One limitation of this analysis is that material data is fatigue material data is limited to aluminum as fatigue data for the carbon fiber composite has not been obtained as that is outside the scope of this thesis. Equation \ref{eq:opt_damage} describes the constraint. $D$ is the damage taken in a single mission. $n_\text{missions}$ is the number of missions the UAV should run before failure.
		
		\begin{equation}
			g_4(X)
			= D \, n_{\text{missions}} - 1
			\le 0
			\label{eq:opt_damage}
		\end{equation}
		
		\begin{figure}[htb]
			\centering
			\includegraphics[scale=0.06]{Images/DFDs/DFDDamage.png}
			\caption{DFD of the Transient Solver} 
			\label{fig:dfd_damage}
		\end{figure}
		
	\subsubsection{Energy} \label{sss:energy}
	
		The objective of this constraint is to keep battery energy above desired levels for the duration of the mission. The design flow diagram for the energy constraint is shown in Figure \ref{fig:dfd_energy}. The energy constraint uses the energy consumption calculation in Subsection \ref{ss:energy_consumption_calc} to determine the total energy consumed during the mission. $E_{used}$ is the total energy used during the mission, it is equal to $E_{Capacity} - E_{Remaining}(t)$ where t is the full duration of the power mission profile. $E_{Capacity}$ is the total storage of the battery. $E_{\%remaining}$ is a parameter which is equal to the amount of percent energy remaining desired after landing. 
		
		\begin{equation}
			g_5(X)
			= \frac{E_{\text{used}}}{E_{\text{capacity}}} + E_{\%\text{remaining}} - 1 
			\le 0
			\label{eq:opt_energy}
		\end{equation}
		
		\begin{figure}[htb]
			\centering
			\includegraphics[scale=0.1]{Images/DFDs/DFDEnergy.png}
			\caption{DFD of the Energy Constraint Calculation} 
			\label{fig:dfd_energy}
		\end{figure}
